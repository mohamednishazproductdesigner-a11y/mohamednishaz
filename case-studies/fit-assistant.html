Feature design

Fit Assistant

A four-step size finder for a women's fashion store. It recommends the size that shoppers with a similar body bought and kept, then selects it for you on the product page.

My role
Product Designer: research, flows, UI, prototype
Platform
Responsive web, as a modal on the product page
Deliverables
User flow, high-fidelity screens, interactive prototype
Tools
Figma, HTML/JS prototype
01 The problem

Shoppers couldn't tell which size would fit

Not finding the right size was one of the most common pain points for online shoppers. People guessed, ordered two sizes, or left the product page. A size chart didn't help much: it maps measurements to a label, but it says nothing about how a particular cut sits on a real body.

For shoppers

Doubt at the size picker, then the hassle of sending things back.

For the business

Size-related returns and carts abandoned on the product page.

How might wehelp a shopper pick the right size with confidence, without a size chart or a tape measure for every number?
02 Research and insights

People trust shoppers like them more than a chart

I looked at shopper feedback, return reasons and the size tools on competing fashion sites. Five findings shaped the design.

What we learned
01Shoppers trust people with a similar body more than a size table.Lead the result with social proof: "90% of people like you bought size 10".
02Many shoppers don't know their exact measurements.Start with illustrated body shapes, and offer "Average / I don't know" as a safe default.
03Fit is personal: some like a snug cut, others a relaxed one.Add a fit-preference slider alongside the body data.
04Long forms feel like a chore in the middle of a purchase.Keep it to four short steps with a visible progress bar.
05Shoppers think in centimetres or inches, depending on where they grew up.Give every measurement its own unit switch that converts the value.

04 User flow

Pictures first, numbers last

The flow is short and linear. The easy, visual choices come first and the typed measurements come last, so the shopper has already invested a little when the effort goes up. Close (X or Esc) returns to the product page from any step.

A modal, not a new page

The shopper never leaves the product they're looking at.

Pictures before numbers

Choosing a body shape is faster and feels less personal than typing a hip measurement.

One answer, one runner-up

Showing the next size with its share explains the confidence without a full chart.

Close the loop

The answer is applied to the size picker, so the next tap is Add to bag.

05 The solution

Four short steps to a size you can trust

Fit Assistant opens from a card right under the size picker. Here is each step and the thinking behind it.

1

Belly shape

Three illustrated side profiles, shown as possible shapes for the shopper's height and weight.

  • "Average / I don't know" is preselected, so an unsure shopper can tap Continue.
  • Dashed guides show the difference between shapes without numbers.
  • Labels describe the shape and never judge it: Flatter, Average, Curvier.
2

Hip shape

The same pattern as step 1, viewed from the front. Repeating the layout means there is nothing new to learn.

  • Straighter, Average / I don't know, and Wider.
  • A selected card gets a yellow border and a check mark, so the choice reads at a glance.
  • Back appears from step 2 on, so earlier answers are easy to change.
3

Fit preference and age

Two people with the same body can want different sizes. A slider from Tighter to Looser captures that.

  • Five stops, with a live label above the handle such as "Slightly loose".
  • You can drag it, tap the track or use the arrow keys.
  • The screen says why age is asked: it helps recommend the right size.
4

Measurements

Height, chest and waist come last, once the shopper is invested.

  • Each field has its own cm/inches switch. The value converts, so nothing is retyped.
  • Pressing Continue checks each field and shows the error under it, in the unit the shopper picked.
  • Values are checked against realistic ranges, which catches typos such as 680 for 68.
✓

The recommendation

The headline is the answer people came for: a big size number, framed as what people like you bought.

  • A bar compares it with the next size, so the confidence is visible.
  • One line explains the data: the share who bought this size and didn't return it.
  • "Edit details" goes back to the inputs. "Continue shopping" applies the size.
06 On the product page

From a question to a selected size

When the shopper returns, the size picker already shows their size with a "Your fit" tag. The card that asked the question now shows the answer and how many similar shoppers kept that size.

07 Usability and accessibility

Easy to finish, easy to undo

  • Safe defaultsUnsure shoppers can keep going without guessing.
  • Clear progressA progress bar and "Step 2 of 4" on every screen.
  • Keyboard readyArrow keys move the slider and Esc closes the modal.
  • Body-neutral wordsShapes are described, never judged.
  • ResponsiveThe modal adapts to phone widths, where most fashion browsing happens.
  • To improve: button contrastWhite text on brand yellow falls short of WCAG AA. A dark label fixes it.
08 Learnings and next steps

What I'd carry forward

"People like you" persuades more than a size table.

Social proof turns an abstract chart into a decision someone like you already made.

Letting people say "I don't know" keeps them moving.

An honest default is still useful data, and it beats a wrong guess or a shopper who leaves.

The explanation has to match the answer exactly.

An early draft said "Size XL" under a UK size 08 result. In a feature built on trust, the copy has to be as precise as the data.

Next steps

  1. A/B test Fit Assistant against the size chart on the best-selling product pages.
  2. Remember the shopper's answers and show their size on every product page.
  3. Add per-garment notes, such as "runs small", using return data.
  4. Fix button contrast to meet WCAG AA.
